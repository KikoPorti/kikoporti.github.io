Computational Models

Using large language models as experimental mirrors — probing where their predictions align with human judgments, and where they don't.

Context

Large language models are trained on text, not on lived linguistic experience, yet they produce remarkably human-like predictions much of the time. That makes them useful not just as tools but as objects of study: when a model's behavior diverges from a human speaker's, that divergence can be diagnostic of what the model is missing — and, by contrast, of what human linguistic competence actually depends on. I'm particularly interested in cases involving Spanish, dialectal variation, and the kind of 'imperfect' language production that native speakers use fluently but that models tend to flatten or over-correct.

My approach

My work compares error correction and interpretation behavior across GPT-4, Gemini, and LLaMA against native Spanish speakers, looking for systematic asymmetries — cases where models 'hallucinate' a normative correction that doesn't match how people actually process or accept a construction. This sits alongside my thesis work using LLM surprisal as a proxy for predictability in both reading and acquisition contexts.
